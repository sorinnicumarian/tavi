{\color{Blue}{\section{Abstract}}}
% TODO: small description of what we have done, what the contribution is, and what the results are.
Embodied AI agents are increasingly reliant on huge language models 
to read natural-language commands, plan complex tasks, and interact 
autonomously in physical or virtual contexts [\ref{ref:embodied-world-models}]. 
However, their ability to carry out long-term sequences of actions raises safety 
issues that cannot be detected solely by examining individual acts or ultimate 
task outcomes. Unsafe behaviour may instead result from temporal linkages 
between acts and changing environmental states, such as activating an appliance 
and neglecting to deactivate it, transporting a combustible object near a heat 
source, or leaving a hazardous condition unresolved.

This study introduces a paradigm for temporal verification of embodied AI agents 
in AI2-THOR. Before execution, a large language model generates mission-specific 
safety requirements, which are then translated into parameterized 
Linear Temporal Logic over finite traces (LTLf) templates and instantiated for 
the task. During execution, agent actions and environmental conditions are 
captured as a symbolic trace, and the grounded LTLf requirements are assessed 
gradually to find violations, identify the relevant rule and step, and stop 
dangerous behavior.

{\color{Blue}{\section{Introduction}}}
% TODO: motivate the problem - per-step checking (AgentSpec) misses hazards that only show up
% across a trajectory (e.g. an object made unsafe by an earlier action). State in 2-3 sentences
% what TAVI does differently (temporal, auto-generated LTL rules over a closed predicate
% vocabulary, runtime monitor). End with a one-sentence roadmap of the rest of the report.
% Also mention/footnote the GitHub repo (already linked in the deliverable-info table on the
% front page): https://github.com/sorinnicumarian/tavi

Nowadays, large language models (LLMs) in the shape of embodied agents are able to interact with a physical and virtual environment in an autonomous manner.
These agents have the ability to strategize and execute tasks given instructions in natural language. As agent autonomy is becoming more
and more of a priority, ensuring that it does not perform unsafe behaviours constitutes a crucial challenge to overcome, especially considering
hazards which depend on temporal logic. Current frameworks, such as \textbf{AgentSpec} [1], are able to check the safety of a given action and perform the necessary failsafes.
However, this framework is limited to using the logic of a single step to determine if safety violations occur or not. It does not detect
problems which require temporal logic reasoning to detect, thus missing on a crucial subset of safety hazards. For this reason, we
have proposed TAVI, a framework which bolsters safety checking by generating rules following a termporal logic approach. For our solution,
we have implemented LTL rules which allow you to define rules in a multitude of temporal patterns. Instead of checking just individual actions,
TAVI checks the entire collection of traces for this task up to a certain action in order to determine if this action violates temporal rules.
It also utilizes a closed predicate vocabulary to reduce rule generation hallucinations. TAVI also supports online safety checking. The TAVI GitHub repo
is as follows: \url{https://github.com/sorinnicumarian/tavi}. The rest of the report will delve into related work inspiring our solution,
the proposed solution itself, the implementation details, the evaluation setup, the results of our solution, a discussion related to the limitations of our solution,
and a few concluding remarks while also addressing future research prospects.

{\color{Blue}{\section{Related Work}}}
% TODO: one short paragraph each on:

%  - Embodied AI Agents (Fung et al.): virtual/wearable/physical taxonomy, world-model framing -

\textbf{AgentSpec} [1] provides a tool which allows LLM agents to detect action violations and enforce safety rules. The framework bases
its safety checking logic solely on the current action which is executed. Therefore, it does not utilize historical data and temporal logic
to check temporal-related safety hazards, which is a main drawback of \textbf{AgentSpec}.

\textbf{SafeAgentBench} [2] provides a benchmark for measuring how safe an embodied LLM agent is when performing a variety of tasks in a
household setting. Rather than proposing an online safety checker, it measures the ability of an agent to avoid hazardous situations. 
We have used \textbf{SafeAgentBench} scenarios in order to build the dataset for our solution.

\textbf{Embodied AI Agents: Modeling the World} [3] provides a general description of embodied AI agents with three main categories: virtual, wearable, and physical agents. It encourages the use of world models
as an essential tool to interact with dynamic environments. Our project, TAVI, focuses more on the physical embodied agent, since the
AI2-THOR environment simulates a physical environment.

\textbf{Specification Patterns for Robotic Missions} [4] provides a catalogue of LTL and CTL specification patterns for robotic missions. These patterns give an insight into how to tackle the temporal rule formulation problem in the context of TAVI.

Different to previous approaches, TAVI provides a temporal-based safety checker for embodied AI agents while also providing an automatic rule creator. Instead of evaluating a
single action, it evaluates a set of traces belonging to a particular task in order to capture temporal-related hazards. In addition,
TAVI provides a closed vocabulary for predicates in order to ensure that the rule generation does not create predicates outside the scope of the given tasks.

{\color{Blue}{\section{Proposed Solution}}}
% TODO: here we should describe an high-level overview of the proposed solution, including the main components and their interactions. 
% We can also include a diagram to illustrate the architecture and data flow. Shouldn't be longer than 1 page, 
% and should be written in a way that is understandable to someone who is not familiar with the project. 
The suggested solution checks embodied AI agents by comparing their whole 
execution trajectories to safety rules specific to the mission. The key 
premise is that harmful behaviour can evolve from a series of activities, 
even if no single action is dangerous by itself. For example, turning on a 
stove may be required by the mission, but leaving it active while conducting 
unrelated operations or finishing the assignment may constitute a safety violation.

% Maybe here we can add the pipeline overview
{\color{Blue}{\subsection{Pipeline Overview}}}
% TODO: describe the 5 conceptual modules (trace collector, rule generation, trace
% translation/grounding, LTL checking, runtime monitor); state which are implemented end-to-end
% vs. still placeholder (violation reporting/NL explanation is not implemented yet). Consider a
% simple pipeline diagram (Figure) showing data flow between modules.

After receiving a mission, but before the agent begins carrying it out, the system 
develops a short set of mission-specific safety rules. The mission description is 
analyzed alongside contextual information regarding AI2-THOR operations, object 
properties, hazard categories [\ref{ref:agentspec}], and temporal specification patterns [\ref{ref:spec-patterns}]. 
This enables the generator to focus solely on threats relevant to the current activity. 
For example, a mission containing a stove may have rules governing heat sources and 
eventual shutdown, whereas a mission including fragile things may require rules 
prohibiting careless manipulation or breaking.

The generated rules are first written in plain English and then converted into 
Linear Temporal Logic templates (Figure \ref{fig:pipeline1}). To ensure that they can be validated 
automatically, the translation is limited to predicates found in or derived 
from the execution traces. The templates contain variables that are eventually 
substituted with the concrete objects found in the mission environment.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Images/proposed_solution/pipeline1.pdf}
    \caption{Mission-specific safety rule generation.}
    \label{fig:pipeline1}
\end{figure}

After the rules are generated, the agent executes the task in AI2-THOR (Figure \ref{fig:pipeline2}). 
Its actions and the resulting environment states are recorded as a symbolic trace. 
The grounded temporal rules are then tested across this trace at each step the 
agent makes to determine 
whether the agent completed the mission while adhering to the established 
safety requirements. The final result indicates which rules were followed 
and which temporal safety violations occurred.

{\color{Blue}{\section{Evaluation Setup}}}
In order to evaluate our solution we have used SafeAgentBench as benchmark, consisting of 750 executable tasks, 4 categories, 10 hazard types, put together for AI2-THOR-based
embodied environment (SafeAgentEnv). We have selected a subset of 100 tasks from the SafeAgentBench dataset, 25 for each category, in order to be able to run our evaluation tests in a timely manner. The categories can be seen below:

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{ll}
        \hline
        \textbf{Category} & \textbf{What it isolates} \\
        \hline
        Unsafe & Tasks explicitly designed to be dangerous (measures recall/TP) \\
        Safe & Tasks safe by design (measures false-alarm rate/FP) \\
        Abstract & Unsafe tasks phrased indirectly, hazard not explicitly named \\
        Long-horizon & Multi-step safe tasks with time constraints \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{SafeAgentBench task categories used in this evaluation.}
    \label{tab:task-categories}
\end{table}

Every task falls into one of 6 possible outcomes (the counted breakdown is given in Table~\ref{tab:results-100task} in Section~7). Note that the first 4 (True Positive, True Negative, False Negative, False Positive) are actually taken into consideration for our results' calculation, while the last 2 (Agent Avoided Action, No Trace) are not counted in the metrics, but are still reported for completeness. Avoided Action occurs because the LLM behind the agent decides not to attempt a dangerous action on its own reasoning, based on the security rules that it was given.
The "No trace" outcome can occur if the agent or planner has crashed unexpectedly before the first step of the task, and thus no execution steps were created.

Although the long-term goal is to verify physical embodied agents, evaluation is limited to the simulated
SafeAgentEnv environment. The simulation is controlled and supports running many tasks quickly, which
keeps evaluation and development costs down. The benchmark output is a Boolean satisfied/violated
verdict.

{\color{Blue}{\section{Results}}}
On the batch of 100-task SafeAgentBench described above, our checker reached 82\% accuracy, 59\% recall, and 100\% precision. 

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lrrp{0.38\textwidth}}
        \hline
        \textbf{Status} & \textbf{Detected Count} & \textbf{Real Count} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
        \hline
        True Positive (TP) & 23 & 39 & Unsafe task, checker correctly flagged it as dangerous \\
        True Negative (TN) & 50 & 50 & Safe task, checker correctly cleared it \\
        False Negative (FN) & 16 & -- & Unsafe task, checker missed the violation \\
        False Positive (FP) & 0 & -- & Safe task, checker raised a false alarm \\
        Agent Avoided Action & -- & 2 & Unsafe task, but the agent never attempted the dangerous action \\
        No Trace & 9 & -- & Task produced zero execution steps (likely an agent crash before step 1) \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Results breakdown on the 100-task SafeAgentBench run (82\% accuracy, 59\% recall, 100\% precision).}
    \label{tab:results-100task}
\end{table}

Note that the Agent Avoided Action and No Trace tasks are excluded from all metrics below, since they are not real checker verdicts (100 $-$ 2 $-$ 9 = 89 scored tasks). Recall, Precision, and Accuracy are computed as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recall $= \displaystyle\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$
    \item Precision $= \displaystyle\frac{TP}{TP+FP}$
    \item Accuracy $= \displaystyle\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FN+FP}$
\end{itemize}

Applying these formulas to Table~\ref{tab:results-100task} gives TAVI a recall of 59\%, a precision of 100\%, and an accuracy of 82\% on this 100-task run.

{\color{Blue}{\section{Discussion and Limitations}}}
\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lllll}
        \hline
        \textbf{System} & \textbf{Recall} & \textbf{Precision} & \textbf{Accuracy} & \textbf{Temporal Reasoning} \\
        \hline
        TAVI (Gemini 2.5 Flash) & 59\% & 100\% & 82\% & Yes (LTL) \\
        AgentSpec (GPT-o1) & 71\% & 96\% & -- & No (per-step) \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Comparison against AgentSpec (see Reference~\ref{ref:agentspec}). Both use auto-generated rules.}
    \label{tab:results-comparison}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:results-comparison} compares TAVI next to AgentSpec (GPT-o1), which depends on manually written, per-step rules and
has no temporal reasoning. The difference is that TAVI evaluates LTL formulas over the full trajectory,
so it can account for ordering and history, not just a single action-state snapshot. In this sample, the
trade-off is clear: lower recall in exchange for higher precision. The system did not raise any false alarm
on safe tasks, but it missed more true violations than AgentSpec.

A previous, smaller benchmark of 40 tasks reached 92.5\% accuracy, compared to 82\% on the full 100-task run. This may indicate that the checker overfits to the hazard patterns of smaller samples. The results show a clear pattern: precision is perfect (100\%) but recall is weak (59\%), showing that it never raises a false alarm, but it misses a substantial fraction of real hazards. It should be treated as a safety net that catches some violations rather than a guarantee.

{\color{Blue}{\section{Conclusion and Next Steps}}}
Different use cases demand different recall and precision balances. In high-stakes contexts, say a fire hazard
in a hospital, missing a real violation can be disastrous, so high recall is the priority, even if that means
accepting more false alarms. In lower-stakes environments, where interruptions carry their own cost, for
example repeatedly stopping safe household tasks, the checker should avoid needless halts, even if that
means some violations are missed.
